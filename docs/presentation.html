Welcome everyone. Today I'm proposing a minor project focused on improving hearing aid technology using deep learning. Specifically, we'll look at how to personalize speech enhancement based on a user's unique hearing loss profile, known as an audiogram.

The Problem 🦻

  • 466 million people worldwide have disabling hearing loss (WHO).
  • Hearing aids amplify sound, but the #1 complaint is understanding speech in noisy environments.
  • Current noise reduction algorithms are generic — the same math for every user.
  • But every person's hearing loss is different (different frequencies, different severity).
"We personalize amplification per person. Why not personalize noise reduction too?"
🗣️ 🔊 🎧

Generic algorithms fail to account for the unique degradation of auditory signals in individual cochleas.

Despite advances in hearing aids, users still struggle in noisy places like restaurants. Current AI noise reduction is one-size-fits-all. But hearing loss isn't uniform. Some people lose high pitches, others low pitches. If hearing aids are tuned to a person's specific hearing loss for volume, the noise reduction AI should be tuned to them too.

What Is an Audiogram? 📊

  • A chart showing how well you hear at different frequencies.
  • X-axis: Frequency (250Hz to 8000Hz) — low pitches to high pitches.
  • Y-axis: Hearing threshold (dB HL) — how loud a sound needs to be for you to hear it.
  • Normal hearing: 0-25 dB HL across all frequencies.

Every hearing-impaired person has a unique audiogram — our model uses this as input.

This is an audiogram. It maps out hearing thresholds across different frequencies. As you can see, 'Normal' is flat near the top. 'Sloping Loss', very common in aging, drops off at high frequencies. Our system will take this exact curve as an input vector.

Current State of Research 🔬

What Exists ✅

  • Generic DNN denoising: Models like Audatic have restored intelligibility to near-normal levels (Nature Scientific Reports, 2023).
  • Clarity Challenge: Active benchmark series by University of Salford focusing on hearing aid processing.
  • Strong architectures: Conv-TasNet, U-Net.

The Gap We Target 🎯

  • Almost no published system takes the user's specific audiogram as runtime input to adapt noise reduction.
  • Most academic models ignore the <10ms latency constraint needed for real hearing aids.
  • Our focus: The specific intersection of audiogram-conditioned + latency-aware speech enhancement.
While deep learning for noise reduction is booming, there's a gap. Most models are generic. And crucially, many academic models process sound in large chunks, causing delays. If a hearing aid has more than 10ms of delay, the user hears an echo of their own voice. We are focusing on low-latency, personalized models.

"The same model processes speech differently for two different people"

Here is the proposed pipeline. Audio comes in, is converted to a spectrogram, and encoded. The innovation is in the middle: The FiLM layer takes the user's audiogram vector and modulates the CNN's internal features. Then the decoder creates a mask to filter the audio. One model weights, infinite personalized behaviors.

What is FiLM Conditioning? 🎛️

  • FiLM = Feature-wise Linear Modulation.
  • Takes the audiogram as a side input and passes it through an MLP.
  • Generates scale (γ) and shift (β) parameters for the main network.
  • Applies to internal feature maps:
    Output = γ × Features + β

The Analogy 🎵

Think of it like a music equalizer that automatically adjusts its internal processing strategy based on your specific hearing test results, rather than just turning up the volume.

Reference: Perez et al., "FiLM: Visual Reasoning with a General Conditioning Layer" (2017)

FiLM stands for Feature-wise Linear Modulation. It was originally used in computer vision, but it's perfect here. The audiogram generates gamma and beta parameters that scale and shift the audio features inside the neural network. It's an elegant way to inject the user's profile without retraining the model.

Methodology — Three-Model Comparison 🧪

To prove our contribution, we will implement and compare three models:

1. Classical Wiener Filter

The Baseline

  • Traditional DSP approach
  • Estimates noise spectrum, subtracts it
  • Fast, but poor in non-stationary noise (like babble)

2. Generic DNN Denoiser

The Deep Learning Standard

  • CNN/U-Net architecture
  • Learns to predict noise mask from data
  • Same exact processing for every user

3. Audiogram-Personalized DNN

Our Contribution

  • Same architecture + FiLM conditioning
  • Adapts based on audiogram
  • Evaluated strictly for <10ms latency
We need a rigorous comparison. We'll build a standard DSP Wiener filter as a baseline, a standard generic deep learning denoiser, and our proposed personalized denoiser. This isolates the exact benefit of the audiogram conditioning.

Evaluation Strategy 📈

Generic audio metrics (like SNR) don't tell us if a hearing-impaired person can understand the speech. We use hearing-loss-aware metrics.

  • STOI / ESTOI: Objective speech intelligibility (General)
  • HASPI: Hearing Aid Speech Perception Index (Predicts intelligibility for specific hearing loss)
  • HASQI: Hearing Aid Speech Quality Index (Predicts subjective quality)
  • SI-SDR: Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio

Efficiency Metrics ⏱️

  • Algorithmic Latency: Must be ≤ 10ms (Constraint)
  • Parameter Count: Crucial for edge deployment

Tools: We will utilize the pyclarity toolkit, validated against actual clinical data.

Evaluation is critical. We aren't just using standard metrics. We are using HASPI and HASQI, which take the output audio AND the audiogram to predict how well that specific person will hear the result. We also strictly track latency and parameter size for edge deployment feasibility.
